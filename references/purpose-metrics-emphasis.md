# Purpose, metrics, emphasis, and cases

Use for Results/Discussion reviews involving unclear analytical aims, tables or figures that list
many values without a finding, unexplained metrics, arbitrary visual emphasis, or isolated examples.
These decision rules were prompted by CDW-policy manuscript comments. The policy examples illustrate
their application; they are not a mandatory research design or a template for other disciplines.

## 1. Align the framework, comparisons, and answers

When the task concerns a full Results chapter or its connection with Methods, distinguish:

| Role | Information it should supply |
|---|---|
| Classification or conceptual framework | What the categories represent and the rules distinguishing them |
| Analysis method | What is compared, the unit and groups, metric and denominator, and the purpose |
| Results | The observed contrasts, magnitudes, exceptions, and the answer they support |
| Discussion | Meaning relative to prior work, supported explanations, and practical implications |

Place an item where it performs its main job. Explain multi-label classification once with the
framework; define the resulting coverage denominator with the metric. Avoid repeating both passages
in every analysis. Preserve a concise reminder when omission would change the interpretation.

For a multi-section chapter, a brief opening can connect the analytical questions. Each subsection
still needs its own purpose. Its answer should become more specific than "differences exist".
Do not repeat an identical purpose sentence or list every subsequent figure in the opening.

Before prescribing more analysis, check whether existing results already provide the missing
comparison. Separate a missing explanation from an uncomputed result. Additional calculations are
proposals until authorized and run; do not invent findings to satisfy a request for deeper analysis.

## 2. Select comparisons with matching meaning

Compare the same metric across groups under matching periods and definitions, or the same group
across periods under a stable definition. Explain a material change in denominator or composition.
Do not compare a whole-period central proportion with a recent-period local proportion as if they
were the same comparison. A pooled association does not establish its evolution through time.

In spatial descriptions, identify actual places and contrasts from source data. Policy volume,
number of active years, and continuity answer different questions. Match observation windows or
account for their unequal lengths before comparing continuity. An incomplete final year remains in
the corpus when that is the study design; distinguish it in full-year comparisons.

Choose examples because they establish a contrast or exception relevant to the question, not merely
because they are the highest values or convenient to describe. First identify the study domain and
the subsection's purpose, then assess each proposed grouping's contribution. Do not require every
possible group, period, place, or interaction to be narrated.

For policy results, a useful sequence is change within the same government level over time, the
same-period comparison across levels, and the concrete task or tool pattern established together.
For example, compare resource utilization, reduction, transport, and enforcement under matching
periods and denominators, then state each level's observed policy emphasis. Do not infer failed
central-to-local transmission from a lower local percentage alone. Separate a missing explanation
of existing evidence from an analysis that has not been performed.

## 3. Explain the metric's analytical role

Before explaining a difficult indicator name, check whether the label itself is necessary. Use the
selection steps in [Policy Results in Domain Language](policy-results-domain-language.md) for policy
studies; use the corresponding field's objects and accepted terms elsewhere. A concrete label must
preserve the metric's formula, unit, and analytical role.

At first substantive use, state the object, unit, denominator or reference, and what a higher value
means. Keep the calculation in Methods and explain only what is needed to interpret the finding.
Replace a label such as "breadth" with its measured object: for example, the number or share of
support categories contained in an average policy. Preserve the actual formula and scale.

For policy-content figures, distinguish these roles when the study uses them:

| Metric | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Policy or co-occurrence count | Number of sampled documents containing the provision or combination |
| Provision coverage | Share of documents in the specified group containing that provision |
| Mean of binary category indicators | Range of categories included per document, expressed on the defined scale |
| Observed/expected ratio | Relative concentration compared with the stated independence reference |
| Explicit citation | A written document link under the study's citation-identification rules |

A small denominator makes a percentage sensitive to individual observations; it does not inherently
inflate or invalidate the percentage. Use the denominator or companion count when needed to judge a
key contrast, and avoid repeated arithmetic warnings after every finding. High O/E with few documents
and many documents with O/E near one represent different patterns. Neither replaces the other.

Different category scores can share the same averaging algorithm while measuring different content.
Do not turn them into a ranking of adequacy, policy strength, or implementation merely because both
range from zero to one. Where categories overlap, a tool-related subgroup score is a descriptive
association, not evidence that the tool independently caused the support expansion.

### Interpret score, ordering, and classification changes

A numerical change should resolve a substantive judgment, not end with "differences exist" or
"the results are important." Use the metric's actual meaning:

| Change | Explain | Do not substitute |
|---|---|---|
| Score increases or decreases | Which aspect becomes larger or smaller under the stated comparison, and whether that changes the scientific assessment | A model-setting contrast described as measured improvement over time |
| Ordering changes | Which objects become more or less prominent relative to the comparison set, and what concern an aggregate ordering could overlook | A number of rank positions treated as physical quantities or an absolute service deficit |
| Threshold classification changes | Which objects cross the stated boundary and why that matters to the screening or decision rule | A similar total count treated as an unchanged membership list |
| Sampled membership frequency or percentile range changes | Persistence of the classification versus variation in magnitude across the tested settings | A sampled frequency treated as event probability, or a percentile range called a confidence interval without a supporting design |

For example, a lower membership frequency under a different missing-data treatment can still leave
an area frequently selected. Explain whether the treatment changes the decision to keep that area
under review. Likewise, a high ordering correlation can coexist with several category changes near
a threshold: it supports broad ordering consistency, not identical classifications. Similar overall
ordering can also conceal a task-specific weakness exposed by matching corresponding dimensions.
Check the component results before naming that weakness or its explanation.

Connect a main contrast to the most specific implication supported by the evidence: retaining an
object for continuing review, checking a particular input, or examining a named resource function.
Give the decision-relevant comparison priority over narrating every value. Explain a mechanism only
when the model, a controlled comparison, or verified evidence supports it; otherwise identify the
unresolved cause outside the manuscript candidate. These distinctions apply when the metrics are
actually used, not as a mandatory checklist for every paragraph.

## 4. Make visual emphasis follow the argument

For each emphasized label, cell, point, or line, identify the specific finding developed in the prose
and its contribution to the subsection's question. Start a revision by removing arbitrary emphasis.
Do not invent a discussion just to justify a bold label. Restore emphasis only for a comparison that
the narrative actually develops and needs. A high value may be less relevant than a gap or change;
do not automatically emphasize maxima. Honor an explicit request to remove all in-figure bold.

Use a consistent meaning for emphasis within the figure and preserve the user's approved style.
Do not add colored frames, a new palette, or extra panels solely to make the figure appear
analytical. An explicitly requested legend frame is a clarity edit, not data emphasis. A quantity
legend shared by differently colored groups should use neutral markers. Figure-reference typography
and data-mark emphasis are separate choices.

Keep size, color, units, and comparison scope consistent with the actual metric. With independent
node-size scales across groups, node area compares counts only within each group. A shared color
scale permits across-group comparison of that color metric, not of the separately scaled areas.
A size legend should not appear to belong to only one category when it applies to several.

## 5. Give examples an analytical role

Introduce a case after the general finding it illustrates, or introduce a specific case-level
question when the broader pattern has not been established. Then identify what the case adds and
the scope of that evidence. A selected case illustrates a provision or mechanism; representativeness
requires a sampling basis. Do not derive a nationwide pattern from the example's wording.

For linked policy clauses, compare the same issue in both documents: central requirement, local
added specification, and the resulting actor, action, procedure, information record, or cost item.
Summarize repeated arrangements in the main text and keep full clauses and sources in a verified
supplement when appropriate. Avoid repeating the same case in Results and Discussion at full length.

## 6. Keep policy inferences at the resolution of the evidence

Policy counts describe issuance activity; provision coverage describes textual priorities;
citations describe written references; paired clauses describe local specification. Empirical
implementation or effectiveness needs corresponding observations. State the measured relationship
directly rather than writing an exaggerated claim followed by a disclaimer.

For example, central resource-utilization coverage of 100% and a lower local percentage establish
a difference in sampled content under compatible definitions. They alone do not establish failed
transmission. Consider level-specific responsibilities in Discussion and use linked clauses for
the narrower question of local specification. Likewise, spatial citation differences do not rank
regional execution. If implementation is the user's intended outcome, identify the additional
implementation evidence needed rather than relabeling the available indicators.

## 7. Finish the Results-to-Discussion-to-Conclusions chain

Before retaining a conclusion, locate the Results passage and evidence establishing it. Repair a
missing comparison when the underlying result exists; otherwise narrow or remove the conclusion.
Do not introduce a new empirical finding for the first time in Conclusions.

Use Discussion headings that identify the actual substantive issue. Expand a short Discussion by
developing the relationship between a key finding, prior knowledge, and a concrete implication,
not by repeating numerical descriptions. Recommendations need evidence for the stated actor,
action, and resolution; label a proposed direction appropriately rather than claiming tested benefit.

When revisiting an older Discussion, recover useful arguments only after checking them against the
current methods, data, and results. Retain useful literature context; do not restore obsolete values
or superseded claims. Add literature to fill an identifiable argument gap, not to reach a citation
count. Source verification follows the main skill's evidence requirements.
